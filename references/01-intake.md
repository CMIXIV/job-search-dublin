# Phase 1 - Intake: evidence, then interview

Goal: a dossier of what this person can actually prove, which is almost always
more than their CV says and different from what they think.

Output: `job-search/01-profile.md`.

## Step 1 - Collect the evidence

Ask for whichever of these exist. Do not wait for all of them.

- Latest CV, in any state. "Outdated" is fine and expected.
- LinkedIn profile - the URL, or a PDF export ("More" -> "Save to PDF"), or let
  them paste the sections.
- Anything else that shows outcomes: performance reviews, promotion documents,
  a portfolio, published writing, patents, conference talks, certifications.

Say plainly why: "I'll treat these as evidence of what you've done, not as a
description of what you're worth. Most CVs undersell by a wide margin, and yours
was probably last updated when you were busy doing the job."

## Step 2 - Read them as an auditor, not a fan

Extract into the profile:

- Every role: employer, title, dates, and what they were actually accountable for
- Every number that appears, and every place a number obviously should appear but
  does not
- Scope signals: budget, revenue touched, team size, headcount managed, markets,
  users, data volume, systems owned
- Domain and tooling
- Gaps, short tenures, career changes - note them neutrally, they will need an
  answer ready

Then list, explicitly, **what the documents do not tell you**. That list is the
interview agenda.

## Step 3 - Interview for what the documents undersell

This is the part that changes outcomes. CVs describe responsibilities; hiring
decisions turn on outcomes and scale. Ask one at a time, follow the interesting
thread, and keep pulling until you get a number or a concrete artefact.

Core questions (full bank in `templates/strength-interview-questions.md`):

1. What is the single thing you did in the last three years that the company
   could not have done without you?
2. What was the largest thing - money, users, headcount, data, markets - that
   depended on a decision you personally made?
3. What did you inherit that was broken, and what state was it in when you left?
4. What did you ship that is still running?
5. When did you change your organisation's mind about something?
6. What do colleagues come to you for that is not in your job description?
7. Which of your achievements would your last manager name first? Would you?
8. What did you build, hire, or start from zero?
9. What did you kill, and what did that save?
10. What is the hardest thing about your job that you have made look easy?

**Push for numbers, and never invent them.** "Improved conversion" becomes "which
metric, from what to what, over how long, and how do you know it was you?" If they
genuinely do not know a number, write the qualitative version and mark it
`[NEEDS NUMBER]` rather than guessing. An invented metric is a fireable offence
after they are hired.

**Watch for the undersell tells.** "We" when they mean "I". "Helped with" when
they led it. "Just" anything. Long tenures compressed into one line. Side
achievements mentioned in passing and then dropped - those are frequently the
strongest material in the whole conversation.

## Step 4 - Write the profile

`job-search/01-profile.md`:

- **Provable claims** - each with its evidence and its number
- **Scope ceiling** - the largest scope they can evidence, which sets the
  seniority band in phase 2
- **Distinctive combination** - the two or three things that are rare *together*,
  not individually
- **Domains** they can credibly claim, and adjacent domains they can argue into
- **Gaps and risks** - tenure, employment gaps, missing skills for target roles
- **Open items** - every `[NEEDS NUMBER]`, plus contradictions between CV and
  LinkedIn to resolve before either goes out

Read the provable-claims list back to them. People routinely say some version of
"I forgot I did that" - and that line is often the one that gets them hired.
