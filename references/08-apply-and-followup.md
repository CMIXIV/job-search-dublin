# Phase 7b - Applying, advocates and follow-up

## The application package

1. **Tailored CV** from the master (`03-master-cv.md`), ATS-checked.
2. **Cover note, when it will be read.** Some employers ignore them; some
   hiring managers read nothing else. Where the form has a free-text box, use it.
3. **Applied on the company's own board**, not the aggregator, unless the
   aggregator is the only route.
4. **Everything saved** to `job-search/applications/<company>-<role>/`: the JD
   snapshot, the fit score, the exact CV sent, the cover note. Interview prep
   three weeks later depends on knowing precisely what was claimed.

## Cover notes

Short. Three paragraphs, under 200 words, in the person's voice - not a formal
register they would never use in a meeting.

- **Why this role, specifically.** One detail from the JD that a generic note
  could not contain. If nothing in the JD is specific enough to reference, that
  is information about the role.
- **The single strongest proof** that they can do the job, with its number.
- **What they want next**, in a sentence, and a plain closing.

Never open with "I am writing to apply for". Never explain the redundancy in a
cover note - it is neutral information for a screen conversation, not a lead.

## Name the gap before they find it

Every application has one thing that could sink it - a named technology never
used, a domain never worked in, a title one step up. Write it down per role
before applying, in the person's own words.

Then the rule that matters: **do not blur adjacent experience into the thing they
are asking for.** Something similar is not something the same, and anyone
qualified to interview on it will spot the substitution instantly - at which
point the whole application is suspect, not just that claim.

Two honest options: close the gap before the interview, or name it plainly in the
cover note and let them screen on it rather than discover it later. Both work.
Blurring does not.

## Advocates - the highest-leverage move available

An internal advocate changes an application from a document in a queue into a
name a hiring manager already recognises. Before submitting, check whether the
person knows anyone at the company - directly, or through one degree.

If they do, sequence it deliberately: **advocate first, application second, or
both within a day.** A referral that arrives two weeks after the application has
already lost most of its value.

The ask should be easy to say yes to and easy to refuse. Something close to:

> Hi [name] - I saw [company] is hiring a [role]. I'm looking after [company]
> announced redundancies, and it looks like a genuine fit for the [specific
> thing] work I did at [employer]. Would you be willing to flag my application
> internally, or point me to whoever's hiring? Totally fine if not. CV attached
> either way.

More templates in `templates/outreach-templates.md`, including cold outreach to
hiring managers, reconnecting with dormant contacts, and the message to a
recruiter who reached out months ago.

**A LinkedIn invitation note is 200 characters, and the count is measured, not
estimated.** The limit was carried in this skill as 300 and two drafts - one of
590 characters, one of 298 - were written before the person caught it. Both
would have failed silently at the keyboard, at the moment they were pasting a
message to somebody who mattered. So: **count every invitation note
programmatically before it leaves the folder, and put the count in the
heading.**

The second-order rule is the useful one. **An approach that needs more than 200
characters is two messages, not one long one.** Version A buys the connection:
the warm signal, the application named once, one credential, and an ask that
costs nothing to accept. Version B carries the substance and goes after the
connection is accepted, where nothing is capped. Both are in
`templates/outreach-templates.md`.

One thing worth checking on LinkedIn's own help pages rather than from memory,
because it changes the strategy rather than the wording: whether free accounts
also cap *personalised* invitations per month. If they do, notes get spent on the
warmest targets rather than the most overdue ones.

## Telling people you are looking

A redundancy is one of the few moments when asking for help is socially
frictionless. Most people wait too long out of embarrassment, and the cohort
effect works in their favour - if a whole team was let go, everyone in their
network already knows and is inclined to help.

Encourage an early, plain, non-self-pitying announcement to their network, and a
direct message to the twenty people most able to help. Draft both; they send.

## The chase ladder

Fixed, and it ends. Most searches leak time into applications that died weeks ago.

**Day 0.** Applied, logged with the date. The follow-up clock starts here.

**Day 10.** One chase, to the recruiter or hiring manager if a name is available.
Restate interest in one line, add one new piece of evidence, ask about timelines.
Never simply "checking in" - a chase with nothing in it is worse than silence.

**On a cold application, day 10 is a search, not a message.** An aggregator or a
bare ATS - Greenhouse, Workday, iCIMS, Jobvite - leaves no reply-to address, so
the chase as written cannot be executed at all. What day 10 means there is: find
a named human, the hiring manager or the product leader who owns the
requisition, and open a conversation. Agencies and named recruiters are the
exception, and there you chase the person.

The corollary reframes the whole pipeline, so it is worth saying flatly: **an
application submitted with no human attached was never capable of producing a
reply.** Attribution across three weeks of a real search was unambiguous - every
process that reached an interview had a human on it, and none came from a cold
application alone. That is not an argument for chasing harder. It is an argument
for attaching a human before the application, which is what the cold-only
Probability cap in `07-fit-scoring.md` is trying to make visible.

**Day 21.** No reply after that chase means the application alone will not land
it. Escalate: find a referral, or approach the hiring manager directly. If
neither is possible, the role is effectively closed.

**Day 30.** Mark it Passed and reallocate the effort. A dead application left
open inflates the pipeline and hides the need for new targets - which is how a
search quietly stops generating leads while feeling busy.

**Always: one chase, then escalate.** Never a second polite follow-up.

**A repost resets the clock.** If a req the person has already applied to is
re-promoted, reposted or re-dated, that is an observable signal worth acting on:
the employer is still looking, and the existing application may have been missed,
filtered out, or overtaken. It justifies one further contact that would otherwise
have been the forbidden second follow-up - referencing the repost specifically,
not "checking in". Worth re-checking live postings for anything still open.

After a screen or a final round, a short note within 24 hours referencing
something specific from the conversation - and for a final, one concrete thing
they would do in the first 90 days.

## Rejections

Log the stage - no response, screen, interview, final. Where it dies tells you
what to fix, and the fixes are entirely different:

- **No response** - CV, tailoring, ATS, or targeting a band above the evidence
- **Screen** - the story is not landing in the first five minutes, or comp
  expectations are misaligned
- **Interview** - depth on the core requirements, or a specific recurring
  question that needs a prepared answer
- **Final** - usually fit or a stronger internal candidate. Often not fixable,
  and worth saying so rather than sending the person into a spiral of self-review

Ask for feedback once, politely. Most will not give it. The pattern across ten
applications is more reliable than any single response anyway.

**Cluster the rejections by what the roles had in common, not by company.** Stage
analysis tells you where things die; attribute analysis tells you why. If every
role that died late had a people-management component, or a domain the person has
never worked in, or a title one step above their evidence, that is a finding - and
it usually points at something they can prepare rather than something they lack.

Reaching a third round proves the CV, the communication and general competence
all cleared. Companies do not take weak candidates that far. A late, repeated
failure is diagnostic rather than damning, and it is worth naming precisely.

**The under-told answer.** A recurring gap is often a gap in the *story* rather
than the experience. Someone told they lack leadership evidence may have led
without authority for years, mentored people, or built a function from scratch -
and simply never said so. Before concluding the experience is missing, check
whether it is merely untold.

**Close the feedback gap with an advocate.** Recruiters will not tell them why
they lost. Someone inside who likes them will. "What would give you pause about
me for this role?" asked of an advocate is worth more than another application.

**Keep an interview history.** Every company and level where a process ended,
permanently. Re-applying to the same company at the same level shortly after a
rejection is low-value, and a year later nobody remembers. A past rejection at a
higher level is also a coherent story if it comes up - "right person, wrong
level" - but only if it is not a surprise.

## Watch what actually converts

Cold applications and warm routes convert at wildly different rates, and the
tracker will show it within a few weeks. When cold screens are returning nothing
and one warm introduction produced a hiring-manager conversation in 48 hours, the
correct response is to stop adding cold applications and work the warm routes -
referral submissions, replies owed, advocates not yet asked.

Say this out loud when the data supports it. The instinct under pressure is to
add volume, and volume is the thing that just failed.

**When a week's roles are all cold-capped** (see `07-fit-scoring.md`), the finding
is about the search, not the roles. The productive response is to spend that
week's hours on routes rather than on applications: who is reachable at the top
five companies, which dormant contacts are worth waking, which recruiters owe a
reply.

## Inbound during a public layoff decays - do not misread it

If the person's employer has announced redundancies publicly, inbound will spike:
recruiters work announcement lists, and approaches arrive that have nothing to do
with the person's profile or any recent CV work. It feels like validation and it
is mostly timing.

Two consequences worth stating plainly. **Do not attribute the spike to their own
efforts** - a CV rewrite that "clearly worked" may have done nothing, and they
will draw the wrong lesson when the messages stop. **And the window is short** -
inbound decays as the news goes stale, usually within weeks. While it is open,
answer everything quickly and convert it into conversations, because that is the
cheapest warm route they will get all search.

The caution above is about *attribution*, and it holds. The *quality* of inbound
is a separate observation and it held up: three inbound approaches in three days
beat a full morning of cold scanning that produced one live role, and both roles
capable of moving the person's level arrived inbound. That does not license
drawing a line from "I updated my CV" to "my profile is heating up". It does
mean the ordering is settled regardless of cause - answer inbound first, cold
scanning second.

## Ask the one question before disclosing anything

A warm inbound arrives for a well-matched role based in another country. The
right reply is one question - *"is there a remote-from-<country> or equivalent
seat for this?"* - and nothing at all about the person's own constraints.

If the answer is no, the conversation ends there and nothing needed disclosing.
If it is yes, the practicalities are handled at screening, in whatever standing
form the process uses. Volunteering a constraint before knowing whether it is
even in play spends something for nothing, and it is unrecoverable once said.

Keep the recruiter either way. Thank them, hand them the spec of what the person
is looking for, and leave the door open - a recruiter who now knows the target
is worth more than one who got a polite decline.

**And no agency role proceeds until the client is named - before the description
is even scored.** `04-sources.md` states this for CV submission. It extends
earlier than that, because a blind submission is the single route by which a
blocklisted employer, or the person's own current employer, gets reached by
accident. A sector named without a client is not a named client.

## Nothing goes on a slide in the agent's phrasing

A panel asked the person what two terms on his own deck meant. Both were this
agent's words, not his. When somebody is asked to unpack a phrase they did not
write, there is a half-beat of translation before they answer, and a panel
feels it even when it cannot name what it felt.

**Every term in an artefact the person will present live is one they would use
unprompted, or it gets rewritten in their words.** Documents they submit and do
not defend in the room are different - density is fine there. The second-order
consequence is worth taking: fewer slides.

## Interviews and offers

When a screen is booked, prepare from the saved JD and the CV that was actually
sent: the three repeated requirements, the person's evidence for each, the
obvious gap and its honest answer, and their questions for the interviewer.

On offers, they should have the comparison written down - base, bonus, equity,
pension, notice, remote policy - against the floor and target from phase 2. If
they are still in a redundancy process, the value of the package they would give
up belongs in that comparison; see `09-ireland-redundancy.md`.

Do not give legal advice or tell them what to accept. Lay out the numbers and the
trade-offs and let them decide.
